On a summer afternoon, the center of a large city can feel noticeably hotter than the farmland a short drive away. The difference is not just a feeling. Scientists have measured it for decades and given it a name: the urban heat island. As cities grow and summers become more extreme, the effect is moving from a scientific curiosity to a public health concern.

## Where the extra heat comes from

The main cause is the material that cities are built from. Asphalt roads, concrete pavements and dark rooftops absorb a large share of the sunlight that hits them. During the day they store that energy, and after sunset they slowly release it back into the air. This is why the gap between urban and rural temperatures is often widest at night, when the countryside cools quickly and the city does not.

The shape of a city matters too. Tall buildings on narrow streets trap heat and block the wind that might otherwise carry it away. Air conditioners, cars and factories add their own waste heat. And because so much of the ground is sealed, there is little bare soil or vegetation left to cool the surface through evaporation.

## Why it matters

Heat is easy to underestimate because it does not destroy buildings the way a flood or a storm does. Its damage is quieter. Prolonged hot weather puts a strain on the heart and kidneys, and the risk is highest for older people, young children, outdoor workers and anyone without a cool place to rest. Nights that stay warm are especially dangerous, because the body never gets a chance to recover.

The burden is rarely shared equally. Neighborhoods with fewer trees and more pavement tend to be hotter, and they are often the ones with lower incomes and older housing. In many cities, a map of summer temperatures looks strikingly similar to a map of income.

## What cities are trying

The most familiar response is to plant trees. A mature tree shades the ground beneath it and releases water vapor through its leaves, which cools the surrounding air. Parks, green roofs and strips of planting along streets work in a similar way, and they bring other benefits, such as absorbing rainwater during heavy storms.

Another approach is to change the color of the city. Roofs and pavements painted in light, reflective coatings bounce more sunlight back into the sky instead of absorbing it. These "cool roofs" are relatively cheap and can be installed quickly, although critics point out that the benefit depends on the climate and that bright surfaces can create glare.

Some cities are also rethinking how they respond during heatwaves themselves. Measures include opening air-conditioned public buildings as cooling centers, adjusting working hours for outdoor jobs, and checking on vulnerable residents who live alone.

## A long-term problem

None of these measures will cancel the effect entirely. As long as cities are dense, paved and busy, they will be warmer than the land around them. But planners increasingly see heat as something that can be designed for, in the same way that drainage is designed for rain. The choices made now about streets, buildings and trees will shape how livable cities are for decades to come.
